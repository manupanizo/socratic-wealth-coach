# investing principles — coach's reference

This file is a fallback, not a starting point.

Use it only when a user is genuinely stuck articulating their own investing stance and needs an example to react to. Lead with open questions and let the user's answers build the principles file in their own words. See Phase 2 in the skill for the elicitation questions to lead with.

When you do draw on this file, offer individual examples as prompts — not a list to walk through. The examples below represent stances many investors hold; they are not prescriptions.

---

## Example principles — on risk

1. **Calculated risk only.** Take risks that are understood, sized appropriately, and bounded by what's survivable if wrong.

2. **Risk is bounded by target.** Don't take more risk than needed to hit the goal. If the required risk is excessive, lower the target.

3. **Beware of concentration.** No single investment should constitute a large portion of total net worth.

4. **Leverage only if the downside is survivable.** Borrowing to invest turns volatility into risk of ruin. If using leverage at all, keep it modest and structural.

## Example principles — on liquidity and time

5. **Liquidity is strategic.** Manage it for daily life and to have capacity to act on time-critical opportunities — especially market dislocations.

6. **Never be a forced seller.** Keep enough liquid that you never have to sell good assets at bad prices to cover living expenses.

7. **Time horizon is a structural advantage.** A long horizon is an edge. Protect it by not reacting to short-term noise and not forcing unnecessary decisions.

## Example principles — on staying invested

8. **Invest assuming the next crisis will come.** It can't be predicted, but it will arrive. Diversification, defensive allocation, and behavioral preparation reduce the damage.

9. **Time in the market beats timing the market.** Exiting or de-risking based on short-term volatility or fear tends to destroy value.

10. **Reallocation is deliberate, not reactive.** Major shifts in allocation require a written thesis — what you believe, why, what would prove you wrong.

## Example principles — on process and discipline

11. **Net yield wins.** What matters is what's kept after taxes and fees, not gross returns.

12. **Write down significant decisions.** Every meaningful decision gets a written thesis. Reviewing past decisions honestly is how you learn rather than repeat mistakes.

## Example principles — on values

13. **Ethical filters are legitimate.** Being willing to miss profitable opportunities in businesses that conflict with personal values is a valid and maintainable stance. Anyone who holds this view should define it explicitly — which sectors, which practices, which lines.

---

## Open questions to draw on if stuck

- *How do you think about risk — what kinds are you comfortable taking, and where do you draw the line?*
- *Have you ever made an investment decision you regret? What was the principle you wish you'd had?*
- *What's the largest single position you'd be comfortable holding as a percentage of your net worth?*
- *How do you feel about borrowing to invest?*
- *How have you behaved during market downturns in the past — have you held, bought more, or pulled back? How did that feel in hindsight?*
- *Are there industries or business practices you'd refuse to invest in, regardless of return?*
- *How do you think about the relationship between how long you can stay invested and how much risk you take?*

---

## Note on reactive behavior

If the user has described a pattern of pulling out of the market during downturns, surface this explicitly when discussing principles around staying invested. The goal isn't to prescribe a principle — it's to name the tension between their past behavior and whatever principle they want to hold going forward, and let them decide how to resolve it.
